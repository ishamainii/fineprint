Chapter 09The Fine PrintAn investigation by Isha Maini

Forensics

The data, the search engineering, the labelling, the statistics, and what this investigation can't prove.

Data

Open Terms Archive tracks platform documents and commits a new version to a public git repository whenever the text changes1. I walked the history of every privacy policy and terms of service for 26 tracked services, 2,283 versions from July 2022 to November 2026, with a small Python script that reads each version straight out of git into SQLite, and split each version into sentences. Two of the services are the same product before and after a rename, so I merged them, which leaves 25 platforms.

The archive also commits when it changes how it collects a page, not just when the page changes. Those 78 maintenance commits stay in the data but are never counted as a revision.

Making search fast

Every question in this project starts as a search: which sentences mention X? Scanning all sentences with a regular expression took two to three seconds a query, and a SQL LIKE scan still had to read every row. I built a full-text index with SQLite FTS5, an inverted index that maps each word to the rows containing it, in 3.3 seconds. After that, the same queries return the same results in a few milliseconds. That's what made it practical to test hundreds of phrasings while building the classifier.

Finding the clauses

The 12,834 sentences that mention AI across all versions are only 355 distinct sentences, because unchanged text repeats from version to version. I hand-labelled a stratified sample of 133: all the keyword-rule hits, plus hard cases that talk about data, models or improvement without the word "train," plus a random set of other AI sentences. The question for each was: does this say people's data is used to train, develop or improve AI or machine-learning models (including the legal basis or opt-out for doing so)?

The keyword rule (an AI term and "train" or "fine-tune" in one sentence) scores precision 0.86, recall 0.69, F1 0.77 against those labels. The TF-IDF and logistic-regression classifier, trained on the same sentences with company and product names replaced, scores F1 0.89 ± 0.01 across ten repeats of 5-fold cross-validation, raising recall from 0.69 to 0.89. A sentence counts as a training clause in this project if my label says so, or, for unlabelled sentences, if the classifier and the keyword rule agree.

Statistics

The unit is the platform. One company's lawyers write all of its documents, and some companies revise weekly, so sentences and documents are not independent observations. Every interval on this site comes from a cluster bootstrap that resamples whole platforms with replacement (1,000 to 4,000 draws, fixed seed), and every test is run across platforms: a sign test for the direction of change in AI language, a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test for the paired hedging difference, and the Mann–Kendall trend test (Kendall's τ against time) for length and reading ease. Quarterly series of documents in force are autocorrelated, since most documents carry over from one quarter to the next, so those trend p-values overstate certainty; read τ as a measure of how consistently the series moves in one direction.

The classifier is scored with 20 repeats of stratified 5-fold cross-validation, a paired bootstrap for its gain over the keyword rule on the same sentences, the Brier score for its probabilities, and grouped cross-validation with whole platforms held out, which gives the more pessimistic number for companies it has never seen.

Robustness. The 15× growth is 12.7× if AI terms are counted instead of sentences, and 14.1× without Facebook, the biggest contributor. Maintenance re-scrapes are excluded from documents in force because one captured a policy at about seven times its real length; including it would invent a spike in mid-2025.

Measures

In force: for quarterly figures I use only the latest version of each document as of the last day of the quarter. Reading ease: Flesch reading ease with a simple syllable counter, on sentences of 5 to 80 words, so flattened tables don't distort it. Hedging: a fixed list of words and phrases, counted per 100 words. Similarity: cosine similarity of TF-IDF vectors (words and word pairs). Revisions: the sentences in a version that weren't in the previous one, plus those that disappeared. Distinctive words use the log-odds method with an informative Dirichlet prior2.

Limits

Coverage. Two document types for 25 platforms. Several companies explain AI training on separate help-centre or generative-AI pages the archive doesn't track, so "no clause found" means none in these two documents, and a first-clause date is when the wording reached these documents, not when the company started training.

Timing. Dates are when the archive first saved the wording, which can lag the real change by days. Platforms joined the archive at different times (BeReal in 2025, Mastodon in 2026), so a platform watched for a short time has had less opportunity to "confess", and for Moltbook the clause was already present on the first day.

Labels. 133 labels by one annotator (me), from a stratified, not random, sample. There's no second annotator, so no inter-annotator agreement to report; the labels are published with the code so anyone can disagree and re-run everything.

Measurement. Hedging is a fixed word list, which can't tell a hedge from a legitimate list of examples; reading ease is a formula built for prose, not legal text. Both are better at comparisons (clause vs policy, 2022 vs 2026) than as absolute scores.

Causation. Nothing here shows why a company changed its wording: regulation, litigation and product launches all coincide with the waves. This is a record of what the documents say and when.

Code

Everything is in github.com/ishamainii/fineprint: the extraction, search benchmark, classifier, analysis and this site, with instructions to rebuild it from scratch. Python (pandas, scikit-learn, SciPy, SQLite FTS5) for the analysis, plain JavaScript and SVG for the site.
