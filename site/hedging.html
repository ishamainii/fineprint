Chapter 3The Fine Print, by Isha Maini

The Interrogation

Compared with the rest of the very same policy, the sentences about training AI hedge about twice as much. Here is the test, and the words that do it.

In plain terms

When a policy talks about training AI, it gets vaguer. Words like "may" and "such as" leave room for the company to do more than the sentence spells out.

Legal writing hedges for good reasons. A company can't promise exactly how it will use data years from now. But hedging also has an effect on the reader: "we may use your content to improve our models" commits to much less than "we use your content to train our models."

Under the lamp

Pick a platform. Its first training clause is read back with every hedge lit up, and the meter counts hedges per 100 words. The white line on the meter is the same platform's other sentences, so each clause is compared with its own author's house style.

The test

Hedging is counted with a fixed list of words and phrases, fixed before looking at the results: may, might, could, can, such as, including, for example, among other, from time to time, as appropriate, where permitted, to improve, to develop, certain. Only sentences of 5 to 80 words count, so flattened tables and headings don't distort the rate.

The obvious comparison, all clauses against all other sentences, is confounded: platforms with wordier house styles might simply be the ones that wrote clauses. So the comparison is made inside each platform: its training clauses against the other sentences in its own current documents. That gives one paired difference per platform, and the test is across platforms.

1

Training clauses are about twice as hedged as the policies they sit in.

Across the 12 platforms with a clause in force, 9 hedge more in the clause than in the rest of their own policy. If clauses and policies were really hedged the same, a gap this consistent would turn up less than once in 250 tries. The plausible range for "twice" is 1.5 to 2.6 times.

Which words do the hedging

I expected "may" to carry the hedging. It doesn't: training clauses actually use "may" less than the rest of the policy. The difference comes from two other habits. One is illustration, where "such as" and "for example" show up four to five times as often, so the clause lists a few uses without saying the list is complete. The other is open-ended purpose: "to develop" and "to improve" are about ten times more common, turning a specific use of data into a goal with no obvious edge.

Over time

Across every document in force, "may"-type words (may, might, could, can) outnumber "will"-type words (will, shall, must, do not, never) steadily, at about 11 versus 7 per 1,000 words. Inside AI sentences, the "may" rate has fallen as AI language moved from vague possibility to describing features that actually exist.
