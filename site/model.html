Chapter 8The Fine Print, by Isha Maini

The Polygraph

The classifier that finds training clauses, running live in your browser, and an honest account of where it beats keyword search and where it doesn't.

In plain terms

I trained a small program to recognise these clauses. It beats a simple keyword search on the apps it learned from, but it does worse on apps it has never seen.

A keyword search for "train" and "AI" misses many clauses, because companies often write "develop and improve AI" or "machine learning models" instead. So I labelled 133 distinct AI sentences by hand and trained a small model on them. It uses TF-IDF features of words and word pairs and a logistic regression. Company and product names are removed before training, so the model learns how a training clause is worded rather than which company tends to write one.

The model on this page is the trained model itself, exported to run in your browser with the same vocabulary and weights.

1

It finds clauses better than keyword search: an F1 score of 0.89 against 0.77.

F1 balances finding every real clause against flagging wrong ones. A score of 1 is perfect. I scored it 20 times on sentences it hadn't trained on, and it stayed between 0.84 and 0.91.

With only 133 labelled sentences the improvement is not certain. The model is probably better than keyword search on this data, but the uncertainty range on the gain only just stays above zero.

2

On companies it has never seen, it drops to 0.69, worse than keyword search.

To test this I hid every sentence from a few companies during training and scored the model only on those companies.

This is the most important limitation. Ordinary testing lets sentences from the same company appear in both training and test data. Each company has its own writing style, so part of the 0.89 comes from recognising style rather than meaning. For the 25 platforms in this archive, which is all the model is used for, the ordinary score is the relevant one. For a new company the keyword rule is safer. For that reason an unlabelled sentence only counts as a training clause here when the model and the keyword rule agree.

Choosing a threshold

The model outputs a probability. Moving the threshold trades precision against recall. Precision is how much of what it flags is correct, and recall is how many real clauses it finds. These numbers come from one round of 5-fold cross-validation, so every score is on a sentence the model did not train on. One round can be slightly lucky or unlucky. Averaged over twenty rounds, F1 is 0.887.
