Chapter 01The Fine PrintAn investigation by Isha Maini

The Confessions

One platform had a training clause at the end of 2022. By August 2026, thirteen of the twenty-five did. Here is each one, in its own words.

For each platform I found the first saved version of its privacy policy or terms of service containing a training clause: a sentence saying people's information is used to train, develop or improve AI or machine-learning models, or giving the legal basis or opt-out for doing so. What counts is set by hand-labelled examples and a classifier, described in The Polygraph and Forensics.

How much AI language is in force

Counting only the documents actually in force at the end of each quarter, I measured how many sentences mention AI or machine learning per 10,000 words, and how many of those are training clauses. Using documents in force, not every saved copy, stops a platform that revises weekly from counting fifty times.

2

AI sentences in force: 15× the first year's rate.

first four quarters 0.56 vs latest four 8.45 per 10,000 words · 95% CI 8.5–30.1× (bootstrap over platforms) · counting AI terms instead of sentences gives 12.7× · without Facebook, the largest contributor, 14.1×

The interval is wide and lopsided for a reason worth saying out loud: the starting rate is tiny, so the ratio is sensitive to which platforms happen to be in the sample. The direction is not in doubt. Of the 14 platforms whose rate moved, 12 went up (sign test, p = 0.0065).

No confession

A missing clause is not an alibi. These are two document types per platform; several companies describe AI training in separate help-centre or "generative AI" pages that the archive doesn't track, and Meta, for one, announced its training plans in separate notices before the wording reached the tracked Facebook policy.
