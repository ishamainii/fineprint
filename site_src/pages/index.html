The Fine Print

a data investigation

If you post on TikTok, Reddit, LinkedIn or Instagram, the rules you agreed to now let the company use your posts to build AI. Most people never noticed, because the change happened inside a document almost nobody reads. I read all of them, every version since 2022, to find out who changed what, when, and where they put it.

scroll

The case

Every app has a privacy policy and terms of service, and companies rewrite them quietly, sometimes every week. An archive called Open Terms Archive saves a copy every time the wording changes. I took every saved version for 25 platforms, 2,283 documents and almost a million sentences, and went through them like a paper trail.

Here is what I found. Every number below was checked so that one company, or one lucky sample, can't produce it on its own; Forensics has the full method.

1

There's 15 times as much AI language in these policies as in 2022.

In 2022 they had about one sentence on AI for every 18,000 words. Today it's one for every 1,200. The exact multiple depends on which platforms you count, anywhere from 8 to 30 times, but the direction isn't in doubt: it went up at 12 of the 14 platforms where it changed at all, and it's still 14 times with Facebook left out.

2

The sentences about training AI are twice as vague as the rest of the same policy.

They lean on "may", "such as" and "to improve" about twice as often as the company's other sentences. I compared each company with itself, so one wordy legal team can't skew it, and 9 of the 12 did it, which is very unlikely to be chance.

3

The documents grew from 211,000 to 367,000 words, and got harder to read.

Reading every current one once would take you about 25 hours. They got longer in 14 of the last 16 quarters, and their reading-ease score drifted down from about 42 to 38.

4

The typical clause sits halfway down, under a legal heading.

Almost never under anything that says "AI". The most common headings above these sentences are about litigation, legitimate interests and third-party data, so you'd have to know the legal term to go looking.
