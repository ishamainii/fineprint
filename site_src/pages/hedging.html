In plain terms

When a policy talks about training AI, it gets vaguer. Words like "may" and "such as" leave room for the company to do more than the sentence spells out.

Legal writing hedges for good reasons. A company cannot promise exactly how it will use data years from now. Hedging still changes what a reader can rely on. "We may use your content to improve our models" commits to much less than "We use your content to train our models."

Under the lamp

Pick a platform. Its first training clause is read back with every hedge lit up, and the meter counts hedges per 100 words. The white line on the meter is the same platform's other sentences, so each clause is compared with its own author's house style.

The test

Hedging is counted with a list of words and phrases chosen before looking at the results: may, might, could, can, such as, including, for example, among other, from time to time, as appropriate, where permitted, to improve, to develop, certain. Only sentences of 5 to 80 words count, so headings and flattened tables do not distort the rate.

Comparing all clauses with all other sentences would be misleading, because the platforms that wrote clauses might simply have wordier lawyers. So the comparison is made inside each platform. Its training clauses are compared with the other sentences in its own current documents. That gives one difference per platform, and the test runs across platforms.

1

Training clauses are about twice as hedged as the policies they sit in.

Across the 12 platforms with a clause in force, 9 hedge more in the clause than in the rest of their own policy. If clauses and policies were really hedged the same, a gap this consistent would turn up less than once in 250 tries. The plausible range for "twice" is 1.5 to 2.6 times.

Which words do the hedging

I expected "may" to do most of the hedging. It does not. Training clauses use "may" less than the rest of the policy. The gap comes from two other habits. The first is giving examples: "such as" and "for example" appear four to five times as often, so a clause can list a few uses without saying the list is complete. The second is open-ended purpose: "to develop" and "to improve" are about ten times as common, which turns a specific use of data into a goal with no clear limit.

Over time

Across all documents in force, words like may, might, could and can outnumber words like will, shall, must and never, at about 11 against 7 per 1,000 words. That ratio has hardly moved. Inside AI sentences the "may" rate fell from about 15 to 6 per 1,000 words. A likely reason is that early AI sentences described possible uses while later ones describe features that already exist, but I have not tested that explanation.
